萬水千山

In the last month of 2023, I became suddenly aware that my mind was growing dull.

Towards the end of summer, I started seeing a Polish boy and made two new friends. Before I knew it, another year was almost over. I had gradually stopped taking the little white and pale-yellow pills I'd been on for years, the ones that had helped me maintain something resembling an ordinary life. Yet somehow, even without opening the packets, without swallowing a thing, I was going numb all on my own.

This winter was much like the last. I lay in bed doing nothing. I didn't even touch my phone.

Strangely enough, I spoke to my ex this month. Stranger still, we were both so calm.

I told him my boyfriend was good to me. He treated me well, but I couldn't seem to feel anything.

"So you're bored with him," he said. "Is that it?"

I asked how he'd been.

"You never understood," he said. "I'm someone who needs company."

I told him I missed the pho in Taojin, the open-air restaurants along the Pearl River, and the old teahouses in the older parts of the city.

Another year had gone by, he said. So quickly. And he still hadn't met a girl he could take to all those restaurants he loved.

"Chinese people!" I said. "Food is our one true love language!"

These days, though, I told him, I barely cared what I ate. If there was nothing I really wanted, I'd find something, anything, just enough to keep myself going.

"You weren't like this before," he said. "You could spend all day talking about something you wanted to eat if you couldn't have it. Crying, complaining, making such a fuss."

And I thought of that girl, who had once wanted things so terribly.

Earlier that year, I told him, I'd been to an island with one of the few Vietnamese restaurants around. Compared with the countryside where I lived, it seemed almost luxurious. It was a small place run by a couple. The husband was English, the wife Vietnamese. She had once worked as a nurse in Taiwan and spoke a little Mandarin.

And there they were, after all those thousands of miles, running a pho restaurant in Africa.

The husband was funny. He said I sounded American.

Ten thousand rivers, ten thousand mountains.

Across them all, and across them again.

Perhaps that's what love is, I said. Passing through crowds, or places where there isn't another soul. Crossing rivers and mountains, wandering through all that vastness until, beyond the dark willows and bright flowers, another village appears.

That village.

To have travelled ten thousand rivers and ten thousand mountains, only to arrive, at last, in my promised land.

In this small town in northeast Scotland, people often asked if they'd ever come back. I could never tell whether they were asking me or themselves.

Aberdeen, for most young people, is a forgotten place, a place in which one's youth seems somehow confined. There are times when I do not like this town. One might say, rather generously, that it stands apart from the world, a small and quiet corner of human existence. Or one might simply say that it has none of the bustle of London, nor even Edinburgh.

"Everything happened so fast," Joey said.

"Who knows where in this life we'll meet again?" I said.

I remembered going to the amusement park with Joey and Yichi. They told me to keep the tickets somewhere safe. Maybe we'd never come back, they said. But if we came often enough, we could collect points and trade them for prizes.

I reached into my pockets, but the points card was gone, lost to the sea wind.

We didn't know then how soon we'd have to say goodbye.

In May, we exchanged books in the cemetery I visited so often, while the spring rain fell. We laughed beneath the strange light of the night sky and pointed towards a crossing where hardly anyone passed. It looked like a dream, we said. So *dreamcore*. As though we'd wandered into a place that existed only in sleep.

And another year had passed.

A whole year, somehow, like a dream.
