I’ve carried an Oriental magpie on my left shoulder for years. Tattooed into my skin, it has followed me through different places, different seasons, and different versions of myself.

I spent the past few years in Scotland before moving to the West Midlands.

There’s a line from one of my favourite Chinese writers, Xiao Hong (萧红), that I’ve always loved:

> “自由从来不容易，不是一个姿态，一个手势，自由是永恒的克服重力，挣扎向上飞行。”

Freedom has never come easily. It is not a posture, nor a gesture. It is the eternal struggle against gravity, the constant effort to rise and fly.

Earlier this spring, I drew a fortune stick that read:

> “災轗時時退，名顯四方揚。改故重乘祿，昴高福自昌。”

It spoke of misfortunes fading away, old circumstances giving way to new ones, and better days to come.

A few days ago, I visited a temple in London and drew another one.

> “理事圆融泯自他，白云飞去了无遮。
> 海纳百川流不尽，空谷万象是吾家。”

And somehow, all of that was translated into one simple English sentence:

> “Broaden your mind to accept all differences.”

Even though I know a little about 八字 and can read Western birth charts, I still find the coincidences of fate strangely fascinating.

Lately, I’ve been having dreams in which a voice keeps repeating the verses from the fortune stick. I keep encountering similar people, finding myself in strangely familiar situations.

Sometimes it all feels so surreal that I begin to wonder whether I’ve slipped into a distorted version of time and space, whether my dreams and reality have finally traded places, or perhaps that’s simply what they call fate.

Somewhere, deep down, I have this vague feeling that fate has already pointed me towards a certain path. I just haven’t quite figured out where it leads.

我的秋天是这样的秋天。

Lying beneath cold medical machines, having vial after vial of blood drawn. Walking through the streets in the early winter cold. Staring at data, code, and lines on a screen.

Speaking to complete strangers in completely unfamiliar places, slowly becoming someone I barely recognise myself.

At an exhibition, someone took my hand and told me never to sell my time for a price that wasn’t worth it.

I’ve been thinking about those words ever since.

There was sunlight that afternoon.

I went with my 老婆 to light an offering lamp for Guanyin (观音). For some reason, I couldn’t stop crying. The tears kept falling, one after another, onto the prayer cushion beneath me.

I prayed. I bowed.

My mind felt strangely clear, almost empty. I didn’t even know why I was crying.

My mother once told me that when she visited Mount Putuo (普陀山) in her early twenties, she started crying just like that.

Perhaps I’m no different from her.

I keep thinking about the fortune stick, particularly those two lines:

> “白云飞去了无遮，空谷万象是吾家。”

White clouds drifting freely, with nothing standing in their way. An empty valley where everything under heaven can become home.

I used to think that if I couldn’t change other people, all I could do was comfort myself by changing who I was, or at least changing my circumstances.

But even then, I would still feel disappointed.

Perhaps what I really need to change is the way I think about change itself.

From Scotland to the West Midlands, from spring to autumn, I feel as though I’ve been changing constantly, yet somehow still searching for the same thing.

The people I’ve met, the recurring dreams, the fortune sticks, and the unfamiliar version of myself that I’ve slowly become. Sometimes everything feels connected, as if there’s a pattern I’m supposed to recognise. Other times, I can’t make sense of any of it.

I don’t know whether these coincidences mean anything, or whether I’m simply trying to find meaning in them.

And perhaps I don’t need to know just yet.

I still believe that one day, I will become the magpie I’ve carried with me all these years, soaring freely across the open sky.
